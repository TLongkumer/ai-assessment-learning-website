Step 3

How the Assessment Criteria Are Created

The rubric turns AI-supported learning into visible criteria. It does not simply ask whether the final answer is correct. It asks whether the student understood AI output, guided the dialogue, evaluated the AI, used examples, and met the assignment objectives.

30 or 40

Marks can change by assignment, but the criteria stay focused on learning behavior.

Rubric sample

The same structure can be adapted across topics.

The sample rubric is for Buddhist ethics and uses 30 marks. The Codex grading agent uses a 40-mark version for a different assignment. The subject changes, but the assessment question remains the same: did the student use AI to learn?

Core criteria used across the assessment model
Criterion What it checks High performance looks like
Understanding of AI Output Whether the student understands and accurately uses AI explanations. Paraphrases concepts in their own words and applies them later.
Quality of Responses to AI Questions Whether student answers show reasoning, not just fluency. Gives thoughtful, specific, developed answers with examples.
Strategic Questioning and Prompting Whether the student uses prompts to learn intentionally. Asks follow-up questions, requests clarification, and tests ideas.
Alignment with Assignment Objectives Whether the interaction moves toward the stated learning goals. Uses the conversation to work through all major objectives.
Directing and Sustaining the Conversation Whether the student leads the process or lets AI lead everything. Introduces angles, keeps focus, and deepens the discussion.
Critical Reflection and Evaluation Whether the student treats AI output as something to examine. Questions, critiques, compares, refines, or corrects AI output.
Use of Evidence and Examples Whether examples are used analytically. Connects real cases clearly to the topic and concepts.

Creating your own assessment

The criteria come from the assignment objectives.

1

Start with objectives

List the concepts, applications, comparisons, and reflections students should demonstrate.

2

Define interaction behaviors

Decide what strong AI use should show: questions, clarification, critique, examples, and transfer.

3

Write performance levels

Describe excellent, good, fair, and poor evidence for each criterion.

4

Add caps for weak AI use

Prevent polished passive work from receiving high marks without evidence of learning.

Important distinction

Correct content is not enough.

A student can produce fluent writing and still show weak AI-supported learning. High marks require visible agency: the student must direct the learning process, ask meaningful questions, critically evaluate AI output, and apply new understanding later in the interaction.
